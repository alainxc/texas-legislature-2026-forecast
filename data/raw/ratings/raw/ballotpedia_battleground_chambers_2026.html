Eighty-eight state legislative chambers will hold regularly-scheduled elections in 2026. Ballotpedia has identified 18 of those chambers as state legislative battlegrounds. Heading into the 2026 elections, Republicans control 10 battleground chambers and Democrats control five. The Alaska State Senate and the Alaska House of Representatives have a multipartisan coalition, although Republicans have an 11-9 numerical majority in the Senate and a 21-14 numerical majority in the House. The Minnesota House of Representatives is tied 67-67.[1]

In even-year election cycles from 2010 to 2024, the average number of chambers that changed party control was eight. The year with the most changes was 2010 (22), and the years with the fewest were 2020 and 2024 (two each). For a full list of all of these changes, click here.

In 2024, 85 state legislative chambers held regularly-scheduled elections. Ballotpedia identified 11 of those chambers as battlegrounds. Republicans gained control of one Democratic-held battleground chamber that year, the Michigan House of Representatives. Republicans also gained seats in the Minnesota House of Representatives, tying the chamber 67-67.

Background

Of the 88 chambers holding regularly-scheduled elections in 2026, Ballotpedia has identified 18 battleground chambers in 10 states. We anticipate that these chambers will be more competitive overall and have the potential to see major shifts in party control.

What is a battleground chamber?

Ballotpedia's approach to determining what is and is not a battleground chamber relies on a series of criteria, including:

  • Partisan control of two or fewer seats would need to change in order to change control of the full chamber.
  • Partisan control of 15% or less of the seats up for election in 2026 would need to change in order to change control of the full chamber.
  • 15% or more of the seats up for election in 2026 were decided by margins of 10% or less the last time they were up for election.
  • Partisan control of 10% or more of the seats up for election in 2026 changed the last time they were up for election.
  • There are more seats up for election in 2026 where partisan control changed the last time the seat was up for election than the number of seats where partisan control would need to change in order to change control of the chamber.
  • The chamber was a battleground chamber in 2024.
  • A battleground state executive official election is taking place in the same state this year.
  • Partisan control of the chamber has changed two or more times in the past 10 years.

Ballotpedia's 16th Annual State Legislative Competitiveness Report analyzes all 6,137 state legislative seats that are up for election on November 3, 2026, in 46 states.

Competitiveness in 2026 is the third-highest since 2010, behind fellow midterm years 2018 (36.1) and 2022 (36.6).

In 2026, the nationwide State Legislative Competitiveness Index is 35.4, above the average of 34.0 between 2010 and 2024.

Compared to 2022, the most recent midterm year and overall most competitive year since 2010, the percentage of open seats is down from 23.8% to 17.3%, the percentage of incumbents in contested primaries is down from 26.8% to 22.8%, and the percentage of seats contested by both major parties in the general election (66.0%) is up from 59.2%, nearly matching the 2018 record of 66.3%.

Key findings of this report include:

  • 1,064 seats were open (17.3%), below the average of 18.7% each year since 2010.
  • 1,156 incumbents faced contested primaries (22.8%), above the average of 21.7% each year since 2010.
  • 4,052 seats are contested by both major parties (66.0%), above the average of 61.4% each year since 2010.

    See also: State government trifectas

    State government trifecta is a term to describe single-party government, when one political party holds the governorship and majorities in both chambers of the state legislature.

    As of November 1, 2026, there are 23 Republican trifectas, 16 Democratic trifectas, and 11 divided governments where neither party holds trifecta control.

    See also: Partisan composition of state legislatures

    From 2010 to 2025, 41 chambers switched control: 21 switched control once, 14 switched control twice, four switched control four times, and two—the Minnesota House of Representatives and New Hampshire House of Representatives—switched control five times. During that same time, there were 58 chambers that did not switch control.

    Before the 2010 elections, Democrats controlled 60 of the country's 99 state legislative chambers, Republicans controlled 37, and neither party controlled the remaining two chambers. After the 2024 elections, Democrats controlled 39 chambers and Republicans controlled 57. Multipartisan majorities controlled the Alaska Senate and House, and the Minnesota House was tied.

    Most changes in partisan control came from major elections, but some also came through special elections (Louisiana House in 2010; Washington Senate in 2017) and party-switching (Louisiana House in 2010; Mississippi Senate in 2011). In some cases, the party that gained control did not have a numerical majority but instead controlled the chamber through a bipartisan coalition (i.e., Alaska House in 2016). This section does not account for changes in party control or ties in a chamber that lasted for less than one year and were not the result of a regularly scheduled election. An example of this is the brief period of Democratic control in the Virginia State Senate in 2014.[4]

    See also: State government trifectas

    A state government trifecta occurs when one political party controls the primary levers of power in a state: the governor's office, the state Senate, and the state House. From 2010 to 2018, the Republican Party increased its number of trifectas and the Democratic Party saw a decline in its trifectas. Democrats picked up six trifectas in the 2018 elections, and Republicans lost four trifectas. Following the 2021 elections, the Democratic trifecta in Virginia became a split government after Republicans gained control of the state House and governorship. After the 2023 elections, the divided government in Louisiana became a Republican trifecta when Jeff Landry (R) won election as governor. After the 2024 elections, Republicans maintained their trifectas, and two Democratic trifectas became divided governments. After 2025, Democrats gained a trifecta in Virginia.
